# Website Copy Improvement Playbook

**An evidence-informed editorial guide for clearer, more distinctive English website content**  
**Prepared from:** *Textstil-von-LLMs.txt*, supplemented with research and primary guidance listed below.  
**Use:** Brief writers, review existing pages, and quality-check AI-assisted drafts.  
**Scope:** This is a content strategy and writing guide, not a forensic test for AI authorship or an audit of your website.

---

## Executive direction

Write for the decision a visitor has to make. Say what you offer, to whom, how it works, what it costs or requires when relevant, and what makes your account credible. Lead with the answer. Replace interchangeable claims with verifiable details. Organize pages so people can find answers quickly without forcing every topic into the same template.

The supplied document describes plausible features of generic AI-assisted prose: repetitive rhythms, adjective-heavy claims, polished positivity, excessive qualifications, stock transitions, and lists that replace explanation. Those observations are useful **as editing prompts, not authorship tests**. An em dash, a word such as “transformative,” or a uniform paragraph cannot establish which model wrote a passage. The source document’s model-specific detection rates and its proposed 85–95% AI-authorship probability should **not** be repeated as established facts: they are not supported by a disclosed, transferable evaluation method. [1, 4, 5]

**Primary outcome:** Readers can understand the offer, trust the claims, and choose a suitable next step. **Secondary outcome:** The copy sounds like a business with real knowledge rather than an interchangeable generated brochure.

## 1. Define the job of each page

Start every page brief with a specific visitor, question, and desired action. A homepage and a product specification page should not answer the same question at the same depth.

| Page | Visitor’s likely question | Essential content | Suitable next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Homepage | “Am I in the right place?” | Offer, audience, concrete differentiation, routes to detail | Explore a relevant service or product |
| Service page | “What will you do for me?” | Deliverables, process, scope, timing, requirements, proof | Request a consultation or proposal |
| Product page | “Will this work for my situation?” | Function, use cases, constraints, integrations, pricing or pricing path | Try, compare, or contact sales |
| About page | “Why should I trust you?” | People, experience, operating principles, evidence | Meet the team or view work |
| Case study | “Have you solved a problem like mine?” | Initial situation, your role, intervention, measured result and caveats | View a related service |
| Pricing page | “What will it cost me?” | Price or pricing method, inclusions, exclusions, terms, next step | Choose a plan or get a quote |
| FAQ/help page | “What happens if…?” | Direct answer first, conditions, exception, next step | Follow the relevant instructions |

**Briefing sentence:** “This page is for [specific visitor] who needs to [decision/task]. It must answer [main question] and enable [next action].”

## 2. Lead with substance, not ceremony

A visitor should find the page’s central answer in the opening. Avoid beginning with general claims about a fast-changing world, industry disruption, or your passion unless those ideas answer the visitor’s question.

**Before:** “In today’s ever-evolving landscape, organizations need innovative solutions that unlock their full potential.”  
**After:** “We help small clinics replace paper intake forms with a secure online process. Here is what implementation involves and what your team will need to provide.”

The second passage is **illustrative, not a factual claim about your business**. Replace its details with verified ones. Google’s people-first guidance asks whether a page provides original information and helps visitors accomplish their goals; it does not prescribe a preferred word count. [2]

A useful opening sequence is: **answer → relevant detail → proof or qualification → next route**. For a complicated offer, the opening can summarize and link to the fuller explanation below.

## 3. Turn vague claims into checkable information

For every impressive-sounding sentence, ask: *What could a customer observe, verify, or expect?*

| Weak draft | Editorial question | More useful direction |
|---|---|---|
| “A seamless onboarding experience” | What steps occur? Who does what? | Name the steps, handoffs, required inputs, and approximate time if known. |
| “Tailored solutions” | What is actually customized? | Identify the options you adapt and the constraints that remain fixed. |
| “Expert team” | In what work, with what relevant background? | Show named experience, credentials, examples, or methods where appropriate. |
| “Save time and money” | Compared with what? How measured? | Give a substantiated result, a bounded example, or a precise mechanism. |
| “End-to-end service” | Where does responsibility begin and end? | List included stages and explicit exclusions. |

**Claim ladder:**

1. **Unsupported praise:** “Industry-leading support.”
2. **Specific process:** “Support requests go directly to the implementation team.”
3. **Supported commitment:** “We reply to support requests within [verified service-level commitment].”
4. **Documented evidence:** “In [defined period], [verified percentage] of requests received a first reply within [time], measured by [method].”

Use the highest rung you can defend. Do not invent metrics or turn a single anecdote into a general promise.

## 4. Make the copy scannable and accessible

Many visitors scan pages to find a relevant answer. Research from Nielsen Norman Group found improved usability for concise, scannable, objective versions of a tested page; its original study is old, so treat its percentages as findings from that study, **not universal performance guarantees**. [3]

- Use headings that describe the section: **“What the monthly plan includes”**, not **“A better way forward.”**
- Put the key idea in the first sentence of a paragraph.
- Keep paragraphs focused; split them when the subject or reader task changes.
- Use a list for steps, options, or comparable items. Use prose to explain causes, trade-offs, or reasoning.
- Make links meaningful in context: **“View implementation requirements”**, not **“Click here.”**
- Explain specialist terms at first use when the intended audience may not know them.
- Give forms and calls to action labels that tell users what will happen.

WCAG 2.2 criterion 2.4.6 requires headings and labels, where provided, to describe topic or purpose. Clear wording supports navigation, including for people with cognitive or visual disabilities; the page’s HTML structure also matters for accessibility. [6]

**Do not mistake scannability for compulsory bullets.** A page consisting entirely of identical three-item lists may be easy to skim yet fail to explain anything.

## 5. Build a voice that belongs to the organization

Choose a few editorial commitments rather than an abstract adjective pile (“bold, authentic, innovative, human”). A practical default voice:

- **Direct:** Speak to the reader when helpful; name the actor and action.
- **Specific:** Prefer observable details to promotional modifiers.
- **Measured:** State supported benefits without superlatives.
- **Candid:** Explain conditions, uncertainty, and limitations that affect a decision.
- **Appropriate:** Match technical depth and formality to the audience and the stakes.

| Situation | Prefer | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Describing a service | “We review your existing process, then deliver a prioritized plan.” | “We leverage a holistic methodology to drive transformation.” |
| Describing a benefit | “Your team can see the status of each request in one place.” | “Unlock unparalleled efficiency.” |
| Explaining a limitation | “This integration requires an active account with [provider].” | Hiding the dependency in a footnote or promising universal compatibility. |
| Inviting contact | “Tell us what you need; we’ll confirm whether we can help.” | “Embark on your journey to excellence today.” |

These examples illustrate **editing choices**. They are not reusable factual claims.

### AI-style tropes: a concrete editing inventory

**Check for these specific patterns.** They are common in generic, AI-assisted web copy and can also appear in human writing. A single occurrence is not a problem or a reliable clue about authorship. Repetition, empty contrast, and absence of specific information are the reasons to edit. The source document calls out punctuation, generic uplift, hedging, and rigid structure; the inventory below makes these observations usable during an actual page review. [1]

| Trope to flag | Typical wording or shape | Why it weakens website copy | Better edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **“Not just X, but Y”** | “We don't just build websites—we build experiences.” / “It's not just a service; it's a partnership.” | Suggests a meaningful contrast without saying what is delivered. | Name the work and result: “We design the site, write the page templates, and train your team to update it.” |
| **“More than X”** | “More than a platform.” / “We're more than a supplier.” | Implies extra value while concealing what it is. | State the additional service or feature explicitly. |
| **Inflated opening** | “In today's fast-paced, ever-evolving digital landscape…” | Delays the answer; could open almost any competitor's page. | Start with the reader's problem or your concrete offer. |
| **“At its core” preamble** | “At its core, our approach is about empowering people.” | Adds abstraction instead of explaining an approach. | Describe what the team actually does first. |
| **Generic praise and uplift** | “Game-changing,” “groundbreaking,” “unparalleled,” “transformative,” “revolutionary.” | Makes a large claim with no comparison or proof. | Specify the change, scope, and evidence; otherwise delete the modifier. |
| **Decorative metaphors** | “A tapestry of solutions,” “navigate the landscape,” “unlock the future,” “embark on a journey.” | Can obscure services and outcomes. | Use literal language unless a metaphor genuinely explains a complex idea. |
| **Adjective stacks** | “A powerful, seamless, innovative, intuitive solution.” | Describes the desired impression, not a capability. | Replace adjectives with functions, constraints, and observable results. |
| **Assumed benefits** | “Save time, boost efficiency, and drive growth.” | Packs several unverified promises into a slogan. | Explain a mechanism and substantiate any performance claim. |
| **Empty duality** | “Whether you're a startup or an enterprise, we've got you covered.” | Pretends different needs receive the same answer. | Identify which sizes or situations you actually serve and how the offer differs. |
| **Mechanical three-part rhythm** | “Simple. Scalable. Secure.” repeated across the site; every section contains exactly three bullets. | Makes distinct pages sound templated; often replaces explanation. | Keep lists when useful; vary structure according to the material. |
| **Question-answer theater** | “Ready to take your business to the next level? Look no further.” | Uses a rhetorical question to launch a sales pitch instead of answering a real question. | Address the visitor's actual concern and offer a proportionate next step. |
| **Unnecessary transitions** | “Furthermore,” “Moreover,” “It's important to note that,” “In conclusion.” | Signals structure without adding information, especially in short pages. | Delete or connect the ideas directly. |
| **Reflexive balance or hedging** | “While there are many perspectives, it's important to consider…” / “It may potentially help…” | Evades a useful answer or hides the conditions that matter. | State the supported answer, then name the real limitation. |
| **Forced positive ending** | Every discussion, including risks and exclusions, ends with an optimistic slogan. | Dilutes essential qualifications. | Finish with the most useful conclusion or next action. |
| **Pseudo-specific proof** | “Studies show…,” “Our customers love…,” “Trusted by thousands,” with no source or definition. | Creates an impression of evidence without providing it. | Add a verifiable source and scope or remove the claim. |

**The em dash deserves its own check.** Its use is normal in English; the problem is using it as an all-purpose shortcut: “We simplify everything—so you can focus on what matters—without the hassle—at every step.” Ask what each dash connects. Would a full stop, colon, or explicit explanation make the point clearer? Check house style and language locale: the conventions for English em dashes and German spaced en dashes differ. **Do not globally delete dashes or treat their presence as proof of AI authorship.** [1, 4, 5]

### Lexical fingerprints: useful leads, not banned words

The supplied document mentions expressions such as “tapestry” and “transformative.” A published study of **scientific English abstracts** found a set of words, including “delve,” “intricate,” and “underscore,” whose increased frequency was likely related to LLM use in that corpus. That is evidence for a **domain-specific frequency trend**, not a universal list of words that identify AI-written website pages. When a conspicuous word recurs across your site, ask whether your audience would naturally use it and whether it conveys a precise meaning. [8]

### Worked website-copy edit

**Generic draft:** “In today's ever-evolving landscape, we're not just a design agency—we're your partner in transformation. We deliver seamless, innovative experiences that unlock your potential. Whether you're a startup or an enterprise, our tailored solutions help you thrive.”

**Editorial diagnosis:** Empty opening; unsupported “not just X, but Y” contrast; em dash doing rhetorical work rather than explanatory work; adjective stack; generic promise; unsupported claim to serve every company size; no deliverables or buying information.

**Better *illustrative* draft:** “We design and build websites for [verified target customers]. Our work includes [confirmed deliverables]. You work with [actual team or contact arrangement], and projects typically begin with [real first step]. See [a real example, scope page, or pricing information] to decide whether we're a fit.”

The brackets are **fact requests**, not suggested inventions. A stronger rewrite needs information from the business; changing punctuation alone will not supply it.

### Fast trope audit for an existing page

1. Highlight every “not just X, but Y,” “more than X,” “in today's…,” and unsupported superlative.
2. Circle each em dash. Check whether it explains a relationship or merely creates dramatic rhythm.
3. Underline every benefit claim. Mark its evidence or the exact process that makes it plausible.
4. Scan headings, lists, and paragraph openings for repeated templates.
5. Rewrite the first screen using approved facts; then remove recurring filler throughout the page.
6. Read the revision for clarity and brand fit. **Do not replace one formula with another.**

The test is not “Does the copy look AI-written?” It is “Does this line answer a visitor's question or make a supported promise?”

## 6. Give the reader reasons to trust the page

Prioritize evidence that fits the claim:

- **Offer claims:** deliverables, specifications, scope, eligibility, terms.
- **Performance claims:** defined metric, period, baseline, method, limitations.
- **Experience claims:** relevant projects, team credentials, work samples, named roles.
- **Advice or factual claims:** reliable sources and the date or context when it matters.
- **Testimonials:** authentic quotations, appropriate permission, and enough context to interpret them.

Place proof near the claim it supports. If a claim matters to health, finances, safety, or other consequential decisions, use especially careful sourcing and review. Google’s guidance emphasizes originality, trust, factual accuracy, and first-hand expertise; these are content-quality principles, **not a promise of search rankings**. [2]

Avoid pretending that every story is a success story. A useful case study says what your organization did, what changed, and what else might have contributed to the result.

## 7. Handle uncertainty deliberately

The supplied document flags excessive hedging and uniformly positive language. Both can obstruct the reader. The remedy is **calibration**:

- Say **“We do X”** when that is a verified description.
- Say **“This may help with Y when Z is true”** when outcomes depend on conditions.
- Say **“We do not yet know”** when the evidence is insufficient.
- Do not use a caveat to avoid answering a question you can answer.
- Do not remove a meaningful caveat merely to sound confident.

**Before:** “It might potentially be possible for our system to provide a more streamlined workflow.”  
**After, if verified:** “The system puts requests and approvals in one queue. It does not replace your existing accounting software.”

## 8. Use AI in a controlled editorial workflow

AI can help turn notes into a structure, identify repetition, offer alternate headings, and check whether a page answers the brief. It cannot independently verify your internal processes, customer outcomes, prices, policies, or brand promises.

**Recommended workflow**

1. Collect approved source facts and identify an accountable page owner.
2. State the audience, search or navigation context, page goal, and intended action.
3. Draft from facts, clearly separating confirmed information from suggested phrasing.
4. Review every number, name, qualification, quote, and implied promise against its source.
5. Add first-hand observations and details competitors cannot simply copy.
6. Check accessibility, page structure, links, and consistency with other pages.
7. Obtain human approval and record when the page needs review again.

**Reusable AI drafting prompt**

> Act as a website editor. Draft or revise the page described below for [audience]. Its purpose is [reader task], and the desired next step is [action]. Use only the verified facts supplied after this instruction. Lead with the answer, use descriptive headings, and make claims specific and appropriately qualified. Preserve pricing, scope, limitations, and dependencies. Do not invent metrics, testimonials, credentials, guarantees, customer stories, or product features. Put unanswered factual questions in a separate “Verify before publishing” list rather than filling the gaps. Avoid stock superlatives, repetitive introductions, and unnecessary summaries. Maintain this voice: [voice commitments]. Verified facts: [paste approved material].

**Reusable AI review prompt**

> Audit this page as an editor, not an AI detector. Identify: (1) repeated tropes such as “not just X, but Y,” “in today’s…,” and decorative em dashes; (2) the visitor’s unanswered questions; (3) generic statements that need evidence or detail; (4) potentially unsupported claims; (5) headings that do not describe their sections; (6) awkward repetition; (7) buried limitations; and (8) unclear next steps. Quote the relevant sentence and suggest a fix. Do not infer who wrote the page.

Google says appropriate AI-assisted content is not automatically disallowed; content made primarily to manipulate search rankings violates its spam policies. It also recommends considering process disclosures where readers might reasonably ask how substantial automation was used. [2, 7]

## 9. Do not use AI detection as an editorial score

Perplexity describes how predictable text is to a particular reference model. Sentence-length variation describes one aspect of rhythm. Neither is an authorship certificate; results depend on the text, domain, language, model, and editing process. The supplied document treats several such signals and named-model habits with unjustified certainty. [1]

OpenAI discontinued its public text classifier in 2023, citing a low rate of accuracy; its published evaluation reported both missed AI text and false positives on human text. Stanford researchers found that several detectors disproportionately flagged a set of essays by non-native English writers. These findings do **not** prove all detectors perform identically, but they are strong reasons not to use a detector verdict to approve copy or accuse a writer. [4, 5]

**Measure the page instead:** Can a representative reader explain the offer? Find the relevant detail? Understand the qualification? Complete the intended action? These questions are closer to the website’s purpose.

## 10. Page-level quality-assurance scorecard

Score each item **0 = absent/unclear, 1 = partly achieved, 2 = clear and supported**. Use the scores to prioritize editing, not as a scientifically validated index.

| Criterion | Review question | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Audience and purpose | Is the page clearly for a particular visitor and decision? | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Immediate answer | Is the core answer visible near the start? | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Specificity | Could a competitor reuse this copy by changing only the name? | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Evidence | Are important claims verifiable and proof placed nearby? | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Completeness | Are scope, requirements, costs, exceptions, and limitations addressed where relevant? | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Structure | Can readers find information through descriptive headings and focused paragraphs? | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Voice | Is the tone direct, measured, and consistent without repetitive boilerplate? | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Action | Is the next step clear, accurate, and appropriate to the visitor’s readiness? | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Accuracy | Has a responsible person checked facts, numbers, links, and current policies? | 0 / 1 / 2 |

**Suggested triage:** Fix inaccurate or unsupported claims first. Then repair missing decision-critical information, weak page openings, poor navigation, and generic voice. Editing punctuation comes last.

## 11. Measure improvements with readers and real tasks

Choose a small set of high-value pages and record a baseline. Depending on the page, useful measures may include task completion in a short usability test, whether readers correctly understand the offer and conditions, qualified inquiries, form errors, and recurring questions to customer support. Search traffic and rankings can be useful context but should not stand in for reader understanding.

Test a revision with prompts such as:

- “What does this organization offer, and who is it for?”
- “What would you expect to receive, and what is not included?”
- “What would you do next if you were interested?”
- “Which statement would you want the organization to substantiate?”

If readers cannot answer, change the page—even if an AI detector calls it “human.”

## 12. Publication checklist

- [ ] The opening states the actual offer or answers the page’s main question.
- [ ] The intended audience can recognize their situation.
- [ ] Distinctive details are accurate and approved.
- [ ] Important claims have evidence or appropriate qualification.
- [ ] Costs, dependencies, exclusions, and limitations are not concealed.
- [ ] Headings describe content; links and action labels make sense out of context.
- [ ] Lists make information easier to use rather than replacing explanation.
- [ ] “Not just X, but Y,” “more than X,” and other empty contrasts have been replaced with details where needed.
- [ ] Em dashes, rhetorical questions, and three-part lists serve a clear purpose rather than appearing by habit.
- [ ] Repetition, inflated praise, and empty transitions have been removed.
- [ ] Names, numbers, dates, quotations, and links have been checked.
- [ ] The next step fits the reader’s likely stage of decision-making.
- [ ] A responsible human has approved the final version.
