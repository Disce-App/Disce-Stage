Language Creates Reality

Why we take language philosophy seriously as a company

There are moments when a single sentence divides a life: an "I quit," a "yes," an "I love you." Language marks transitions, clarifies fronts, opens doors, or closes them again. It is not merely a vehicle for transporting information — it is the stage on which we become accountable to ourselves and to others. Whoever learns to speak, learns to think; whoever learns to say what they actually mean, takes responsibility for their own life. This is not a marketing conceit. It is the operating premise on which Disce is built, and it is why the mantra that gives this page its title — Sag, was Du meinst — is treated internally as doctrine, not slogan.

German makes this relationship unusually visible. The language forces decisions: where the verb goes, which nuance to choose — "perhaps," "probably," "I am convinced" — how to compress a complex matter into a single compound noun such as Weltanschauung, Entscheidungsreife, or Sprachlosigkeit. These forms are more than the grammatical rules for which German is famous; they are invitations to perceive the world with greater differentiation, and to move through it more honestly.

Disce understands itself as a bridge across this field — between philosophical insight, academic register, and the real sentences people actually produce in study, work, and daily life. Before any product decision, we return to a more basic question: what does language actually do, in us, between us, and inside our institutions?

Philosophical Foundations

Five thinkers, one operating premise

Language creates reality: what we say, and how we say it, orders perception, guides action, and confers meaning. Precise, courageous speech is an act of both character and competence. German offers particular leverage here — flexible sentence rhythm, powerful compounds, register-sensitive nuance — a tool for differentiated thought and effective participation. We ground this claim in five distinct traditions, each of which contributes a load-bearing structure to our product philosophy rather than a decorative epigraph.

Carl Gustav Jung treats personality maturation as individuation, its stations marked by archetypal symbols; the first step leads through the experience of the "shadow" — the repressed material that contains not only problematic tendencies but also creative impulse and realistic insight. Making the unsayable sayable is, for Jung, the key to psychic wholeness. We take from this that articulation is not incidental to development — it is the mechanism of it.

Ludwig Wittgenstein moves language out of the sphere of abstract meaning and into practice:

"For a large class of cases — though not for all — in which we employ the word 'meaning' it can be defined thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language." (Philosophical Investigations, §43)
What words mean depends on the rules of the relevant language-game and the shared form of life; the same exclamation, "Water!", can be a request, a command, or an answer depending on context. Clarity of expression is therefore a competence — recognizing the rules and purposes of the game one is in — rather than a search for essential definitions.

Wilhelm von Humboldt accentuates the world-forming power of language: it is the "outward manifestation of the spirit of a people"; the differences between languages are, at bottom, "a difference of worldviews themselves." Not a neutral instrument of communication but a "formative organ of thought," language shapes the manner in which we grasp objects, relations, and values. Register, idiom, and grammatical form are therefore load-bearing structures for perception and meaning — every language opens a specific access route to the world. We return to Humboldt at length in the next section, because his framework does more work for our product philosophy than any other single source.

With J. L. Austin and John Searle, speaking becomes explicitly visible as action. Performative utterances such as "I promise" or "I appoint" are not mere descriptions of fact but acts in which the utterance itself performs a deed. Austin distinguishes three levels: the locutionary act (saying something, with sense and reference), the illocutionary act (doing something in saying it — asserting, requesting, warning), and the perlocutionary act (producing effects in the hearer through what was said — persuading, alarming). Searle systematizes speech acts as rule-governed actions dependent on intention, convention, and shared background assumption. Language thereby generates commitment and social reality: promises, judgments, and acknowledgments "hold" by shared convention — to say is to do.

Viktor Frankl and Hartmut Rosa connect language to meaning and to a successful relation with the world. Frankl emphasizes the "will to meaning" and the individual's responsibility: life "asks" us, and the answer occurs not in bare speech but in right action and disposition. Rosa describes acceleration as the defining problem of the present and proposes resonance as the answer: quality of life is measured not primarily by resources, options, or moments of happiness, but by the quality of our relation to the world — successful attunement rather than alienation. Clear, responsible language opens exactly these resonance axes: it creates binding relations, makes meaning experienceable, and anchors action socially.

In sum: language integrates the interior (Jung), structures practice (Wittgenstein), forms worldview (Humboldt), constitutes action (Austin/Searle), and establishes meaning and resonance (Frankl/Rosa). "Say what you mean" is therefore not a stylistic ideal but a disposition — the deliberateness in the choice of word, tone, and form through which personality matures, community is carried, and reality comes into being.

Language as Worldview

From Humboldt's Weltansicht to modern psycholinguistics

Imagine writing the same message twice: once in your first language, once in German. The content is identical, yet something feels different. The German version reads as more structured, perhaps harsher, or conversely, over-polite. A résumé becomes a gapless system of responsibilities. An email to a public authority suddenly reads like a small administrative act. In a seminar paper, one notices the need to unroll a line of reasoning explicitly — "In the following, I will show…" — rather than merely gesturing toward it. Many advanced learners describe precisely this sensation: thinking in German is not simply "thinking in different words," but a consistently different way of sorting the world.

Humboldt would not have found this surprising. For him, language is "the outward appearance of the spirit of a people," language and spirit so tightly interlocked that he can write: "their language is their spirit, and their spirit is their language," and that differences between languages are ultimately a concrete "difference of worldviews themselves." More pointedly: "Language is the formative organ of thought." On this view, language is no neutral cable transporting ready-made thoughts, but the medium in which thought first takes shape at all. To change language is to change one's standpoint on the world — even where the shift is not immediately visible, or is at first only a matter of nuance.

This idea resurfaces under the name of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, or "linguistic relativity": the structure of a first language influences how we perceive, remember, and categorize. We now know from experiment that this is not pure speculation. Studies show that German speakers read motion events more readily from their endpoint than English speakers do; that certain judgment habits shift with second-language acquisition; and that a lifetime's work with a given language leaves a trace in the wiring of the brain's language network. At the same time, these effects are bounded and domain-specific: language does not fix our thought, it weights it.

This shows up concretely in the most mundane German text types: the email to an authority, the job application, the seminar essay. Consider the typical bureaucratic email: "Aufgrund der verspäteten Zustellung meines Bescheides bitte ich um Überprüfung des Vorgangs." Even the surface tells a small theory of the world. Events are treated as processes rather than stories — something with a file number, a status, a history. People appear as applicants, case officers, responsible parties — roles within a system of accountability, deadlines, and obligations. To someone coming from a more narrative or more personalized writing culture, this initially reads as cold and bureaucratic. Read through Humboldt's lens, it reveals a worldview in which order, traceability, and accountability are central — the language forces you to think in exactly these dimensions the moment you write "properly" to a German authority.

This is more pronounced still in job applications, where curriculum vitae and cover letter follow an implicit structural compulsion: unbroken chronology, cleanly labeled positions, formulas like "responsible for…," "in charge of…," "achievements: …" A biography appears not as a loose sequence of experiences but as an ordered system of roles, competencies, and evidence. Learning to write German applications is therefore unavoidably work on one's own self-conception: one learns to see oneself as a bundle of clearly nameable responsibilities and results.

Academic culture reveals a further facet: thought as a cleanly conducted movement. Longer sentences with clear subordination, metasignals such as "In the following, I will show…," "First… then…," and a strict sequence of question–method–result–conclusion model thinking as something that must be unrolled in structured fashion and made traceable for others. Humboldt again: "There is nothing isolated in language; each of its elements announces itself only as part of a whole." To write in German is to accept that no sentence stands as an isolated idea — each linguistic element must be embedded in a larger argumentative or explanatory whole.

For the advanced German learner, this means that drafting an official email, formulating a job application, or structuring an academic text is doing more, in Humboldt's sense, than merely "finding the right words." One practices a different way of reading and sorting the world: treating events as file-numbered processes, narrating a résumé as a structured sequence of roles and responsibilities, building an argument as a clearly guided movement through a topic. Disce treats this not as trivia but as the actual content of advanced-level instruction — a second vantage point on the same world, deliberately acquired rather than accidentally arrived at.

Why German, Why Now

Academic bilingualism and the case for German as a language of scholarship

"German academic language is a historically grown, tradition-rich, and complex asset. It represents an essential resource for scientific insight and creativity and secures both the vitality of the common language and the transmission of scientific results to a non-academic public" (DAAD, 2010). German has, for some time, lost visibility in international science by international comparison, particularly in STEM fields; at the same time, lived multilingualism has been shown to expand rather than curtail insight. The DAAD explicitly advocates academic multilingualism: English as the global working language and German as an independent language of science and mediation — not either/or, but both/and. This is precisely where Disce positions itself: as a digital language tutor from the private sector that translates academic language into professional and civic action.

Historically and systematically, languages order concepts differently — "one gathers new, differently ordered ideas when one studies foreign languages" (Knigge, cited in Eichinger, 2017). A workable frame follows: German as the primary language for definitional work, terminology, and compounding; English for global dissemination, alongside secured German-language accessibility of results — abstracts and executive summaries. Disce operationalizes exactly this bridge to academic register: sharpening terminology and process through the sequence define–justify–conclude.

"English-only" creates distance. Institutions including the Goethe-Institut warn of a growing inarticulacy of German for complex matters, with tangible consequences for popular-science communication and the long-term viability of German conceptual vocabulary. In medicine, public administration, and education particularly, explicability in German is a precondition for trust and accountability. Disce trains exactly this public register of educated speech: clear speech acts — asserting, requesting, promising, explaining — comprehensible prosody, and coherent text structure, so that research remains connective — in the lecture hall, the consultation, the public office, and the media.

"The international attractiveness of a language stands in close relation to the economic, cultural, and scientific significance of the country in which it is spoken. Excellent science is the best advertisement for the German language" (DAAD, 2010). German as a language of scholarship is not retreat but advance: it broadens the perspective of conceptual work, stabilizes the social contract of research, and eases integration. Multilingualism is the mode of modern science, with German as a load-bearing pillar at the heart of Europe. Disce builds the bridge from the academic sphere to public practice: insight becomes clear language, and clear language becomes effect.

On Honest Measurement

Why we reject the single progress score

Imagine a physician who, after a comprehensive examination, tells you only: "Your health is at 73 out of 100." You would not know whether your cardiovascular system is excellent and your liver values concerning, or the reverse. You would have a number, but no knowledge. This is, broadly, how we currently treat language learning. We chase experience points, collect "crowns," maintain daily "streaks," and arrive at a label: "You are B2." But what does that mean? Why does one person with a "solid B2" feel sovereign in meetings, while another with the identical label avoids a call to the tax office the way one avoids a root canal? The problem does not always lie with the learner — it lies in how we measure progress. Conventional metrics are frequently too one-dimensional to capture the complexity of real communicative competence. They manufacture an illusion of progress that collapses exactly when it matters: in the interview, the salary negotiation, the discussion with a professor.

Disce holds that it is time for a more honest, multidimensional diagnostic model — one that measures not only whether a learner is improving, but in what, and why. Our approach integrates four established pillars: the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR), which describes competence as the ability to accomplish communicative tasks in concrete domains rather than as abstract knowledge; the German Kultusministerkonferenz's guidance on academic register (Bildungssprache), which distinguishes explicitly between everyday and educated language and treats the latter as the register required for structured argument, formal communication, and complex subject matter; the ALTE Can-Do scales, which translate CEFR levels into concrete task descriptions and operationalize "can do" as roughly an 80% success probability on a task of the corresponding level — the mastery logic at the core of our scenario-based approach; and the CAF paradigm (Complexity, Accuracy, Fluency) from empirical second-language-acquisition research, which shows that a combination of lexical and grammatical complexity, accuracy, and fluency reliably predicts CEFR level.

In place of a single progress bar, Disce constructs a multidimensional profile across measurable dimensions: pronunciation and prosody; fluency, including filler-word frequency and pause structure; grammatical control, weighted for structural complexity rather than error count alone; lexical breadth and precision, including domain-appropriate collocation; coherence and cohesion; and task fulfillment, pragmatics, and register — whether the tone actually fit the situation. These dimensions are captured separately for speaking, writing, reading, and listening, and visualized in a single dashboard, with explicit uncertainty ("level estimate: B2 ± half a level, further samples recommended") rather than false precision.

Progress, at Disce, is not a static point total but a weekly dialogue: which dimensions improved most, what the success rate is on target-level tasks, and a short verbal summary connecting the data to concrete behavior — "noticeably fewer filler words in summaries, fluency score +10, more room for conclusions." This is more than a technical instrument; it is a lever of applied motivational psychology. Making progress transparent, multidimensional, and goal-relevant builds self-efficacy, converts vague ambition ("get better at German") into concrete, measurable targets, and sustains the kind of intrinsic motivation that abstract point totals cannot.

Culture, Community, and Co-Creation

Why Disce is a platform, not merely an application

The prevailing generation of language apps has made learning more accessible and more entertaining. Yet despite millions of downloads, the pattern is consistent: limited speaking practice, with little genuine interactive communication or community exposure; thin cultural immersion, with dialects, idiom, and tradition largely absent in favor of vocabulary and grammar; an isolated learning process, without real learning groups, debate, or shared projects; an overemphasis on unproductive gamification, where point accumulation displaces genuine motivation; and a one-size-fits-all approach, in which individual goals and interests play almost no role. Learners who rely solely on conventional apps build foundational knowledge but rarely fluent competence or cultural understanding — long-term motivation suffers, and many give up early.

Disce is the deliberate counter-model to language apps built for scale and interchangeability. Our platform is devoted entirely to German — with all its facets, idiosyncrasies, and histories — because language is not a neutral tool like mathematics, but something that lives through the people who speak it. It gains character, depth, and expressive power through diversity and individuality. We understand German as a dynamic, many-faceted system extending far beyond vocabulary and grammar, opening access to distinct registers and domains: business, scientific, and legal German; youth idiom, rhetoric, and philosophical register; media, technical, and medical language; literary and creative expression; intercultural communication and public administration; tourism, gaming, sustainability, and pedagogical register. Each domain of life has its own language, and genuine command requires more than standard phrases — it requires a feel for tone, nuance, and register.

This is reflected in what we consider the non-negotiable differentiators of the platform. Active participation: users are not merely consumers but co-creators, able to contest or propose pronunciations, build and share original learning materials and challenges, submit and discuss new or unusual words, and debate linguistic change, style, and relevance with the community — the platform grows with its members and stays current by construction. Cultural depth: German is understood as more than rules and lists, connecting to the breadth of its linguistic-cultural heritage, from proverbs and dialects to contemporary trends, from Goethe to youth slang. Standards without gatekeeping: low barriers to entry are good, but genuine development requires challenge — Disce positions itself as tutor, mentor, and language companion, with the goal not of "checking off words" but of sovereignty of expression across subject areas, situations, and roles. Productive gamification, oriented on what we internally call the "Animal Crossing paradigm" — a design philosophy borrowed from the game of the same name — favoring long-term motivation, small accomplishments, individualized paths, and creative co-authorship over point totals and hollowed-out leaderboards. And research and trend analysis: anonymized usage data and community contributions surface new terms, tendencies, and cultural shifts, so that users experience language as something they shape together and study together.

The audience this serves is deliberately broad: learners across youth, university, and professional life; teachers and language coaches at schools, universities, and private language institutions; the creative and cultural scene — authors, poetry slammers, content creators, debate societies; researchers and institutions, including linguists, universities, and public bodies; employers using Disce for professional development and employer branding; German-speaking diaspora communities worldwide; and distribution and technology partners extending reach through app stores, learning-management systems, and EdTech networks. Disce is, in short, not simply another language-learning application. It is a living platform and institution of language — one that brings together people who do not merely master German, but live it, shape it, and carry it forward.

Technology in the Service of Sovereignty

Our position on artificial intelligence

Disce uses artificial intelligence to make language learning personal, culturally deep, and communal — not to chase high scores. Our technology posture rests on three commitments: European standards, open source, and digital sovereignty. We begin with open EU models, intend mid-term operation within Europe, and hold a long-term ambition of a proprietary, German-centered AI language tutor.

On the model layer, we are evaluating Teuken-7B from OpenGPT-X — an open, multilingual large language model "made in Europe," trained across all 24 EU languages, with a commercially usable Instruct variant under Apache-2.0. Our architecture begins with EU/EEA cloud infrastructure for the MVP, with modularly interchangeable AI components, and a target state of proprietary GPU resources within Europe for latency, cost control, and data protection.

We are deliberate about what data we collect and why, from day one: core product data — profile, learning progress, flashcard status, pseudonymized test responses, and user-generated content drafts — necessary to function; sparing product analytics limited to essential usage events for UX improvement, with no cross-service tracking; quality and moderation signals such as duplicate detection, readability checks, and abuse flags; and explicit opt-ins for personalization, research participation, and — later — selective fine-tuning, each with a clear purpose, the ability to withdraw, and full transparency.

Our intelligent systems are scoped narrowly and explainably: content recommendation with rarity and topic mixing and duplicate protection, plus a short rationale ("why am I seeing this?"); spaced repetition for flashcards, with "error decks" and optional synonym or example assistance; adaptive testing with light stage logic and percentile comparison against rolling cohorts, moving toward psychometric calibration; moderation and user-generated content review through fuzzy and embedding-based duplicate search and readability heuristics, with humans deciding edge cases; and recommendations oriented on "the next sensible step" rather than high-score pressure, transparent and always deactivable.

On governance, we hold ourselves to European standards: privacy by design under the GDPR, orientation toward the EU AI Act, data minimization, clear opt-ins, and EU/EEA hosting. We commit to explainability and control — labeling of AI-generated content, a standing "why am I seeing this?" answer, and clear channels for objection and appeal. We commit to fairness across linguistic varieties, distinguishing standard and legitimate variant forms rather than erasing the latter, with community-driven whitelisting of variants. And we commit to youth protection through safe default settings, register labeling, and reporting and review mechanisms.

We are equally explicit about what we will not do. We will not make certification promises we cannot keep — Disce does not issue official CEFR certificates. We will not build "pay-to-win" mechanics; monetization stays deliberate and remains cosmetic or functional rather than performance-gating. And we will not make black-box decisions — recommendations are reasoned and contestable. Our roadmap toward a proprietary AI language tutor runs in three phases: an open, European phase built on Teuken-7B with adapters fine-tuned for German-specific tasks; a German-first phase with a proprietary German tokenizer, curated German corpora including dialectal variety, and reinforcement learning from German-language feedback; and a sovereignty phase operating on proprietary EU infrastructure, with on-device inference for select tasks and APIs for partners that do not compromise our privacy principles. Compute, not data, is our intended long-term commercial layer: we do not sell personal data, do not cross-track, and do not personalize via black box — what we intend eventually to offer commercially is European-hosted compute and models, with personal data remaining purpose-bound within Disce.

Synthesis: A Mantra as Institutional Philosophy

What this commits us to as we incorporate

Tracing the path from Humboldt through Sapir–Whorf to contemporary psycholinguistics yields a picture that is neither romantic overstatement of the mother tongue nor casual neuroscience folklore. Humboldt's provocation — that differences between languages are, at bottom, "differences of worldviews" — and its empirical descendants converge on a modest but real claim: the structure of a language shapes perception, memory, and categorization, without foreclosing alternative thought. For the advanced German learner, drafting a bureaucratic email, formulating a job application, or structuring an academic text is doing more than "finding the right words" — it is practicing a different way of reading and sorting the world.

Humboldt wrote that learning a foreign language should be "the acquisition of a new standpoint within one's existing worldview." Learning German at an advanced level, on this account, becomes not merely an additional communication tool but a second vantage point on the same world. Disce understands language in this sense — not merely as an examination subject, but as a practice in which one deliberately readjusts one's thinking, one's role, and one's manner of narrating reality.

"Say what you mean" is, in the end, neither a stylistic ideal nor a company tagline. It is a discipline: the deliberateness in word, tone, and form through which a person matures, a community is carried, and reality is constituted. As we move toward incorporation, this is the standard we hold our product, our team, and our public communication to — including the plain fact that we would rather say a version of this argument at length, and correctly, than compress it into something shorter and less honest.
